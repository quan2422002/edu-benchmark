\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly capable of producing
correct answers and fluent explanations, but these abilities do not by
themselves establish that a model can tutor effectively. Tutoring also
requires \textit{(i)} interpreting what a learner currently understands;
\textit{(ii)} diagnosing errors when evidence permits;
\textit{(iii)} selecting an appropriate pedagogical move;
\textit{(iv)} calibrating the amount of support; and
\textit{(v)} communicating in a way that preserves the learner's
opportunity to think. Recent tutoring benchmarks
support this distinction. MathTutorBench reports that mathematical
problem-solving ability does not directly translate into pedagogical
quality \cite{macina2025mathtutorbench}; KMP-Bench separates verifiable
mathematical skills from principle-guided dialogue quality
\cite{shi2026kmpbench}; and TutorBench evaluates responses with
fine-grained, sample-specific criteria \cite{srinivasa2025tutorbench}.
Evaluating a tutor is therefore an assessment-design problem rather than
only a question-answering problem.

Prior AI-tutoring benchmarks leave three gaps for our target setting.
First, their domain coverage is concentrated in mathematics or broad
STEM curricula, while specialized school subjects remain less explored.
Second, they operate predominantly in English, leaving language- and
curriculum-specific tutoring behavior underrepresented. Third, data
construction is not unified: \textit{(i)} KMP-Bench builds pedagogically
planned dialogue flows; \textit{(ii)} TutorBench uses expert-authored
situations and rubrics; and \textit{(iii)} MathTutorBench assembles
existing tutoring datasets under predefined tasks and metrics. In each
case, prior assumptions about tasks or
pedagogical situations structure the data before evaluation. Less is
known about starting from ordinary, teacher-authored pedagogical
dialogues collected independently of a benchmark taxonomy and converting
them into traceable evaluation units.

We address these gaps by constructing a Vietnamese lower-secondary
Informatics AI-tutor benchmark for Grades 6--9. Rather than generating
conversations from predefined benchmark situations, our process
\textit{(i)} begins with raw teacher-authored dialogues;
\textit{(ii)} applies auditable quality control; and
\textit{(iii)} converts eligible tutor turns into next-response
evaluation candidates. The benchmark then connects six pedagogical principles with
six tutor capabilities to specify what a response should accomplish and
how its quality should be evaluated.

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a traceable construction pipeline that transforms 1,050 raw
    teacher-authored dialogues into 665 quality-controlled dialogue
    families and 2,028 turn-level benchmark candidates;
    \item a measurement specification that combines six pedagogical
    principles with six tutor capabilities through response requirements,
    a common rubric tier, and principle-specific rubric tiers; and
    \item an analysis of pedagogical-requirement coverage and benchmark
    readiness, identifying 1,400 candidates ready for downstream
    construction and 628 candidates routed to targeted review.
\end{enumerate}
Together, these contributions establish a curriculum-grounded and
traceable basis for evaluating AI tutoring responses in Vietnamese
lower-secondary Informatics.

\section{Related Work and Background}

\subsection{Pedagogical and Measurement Foundations}

The pedagogical vocabulary in this work begins with Allison and Tharby's
six principles: \textit{(i)} Challenge; \textit{(ii)} Explanation;
\textit{(iii)} Modelling; \textit{(iv)} Practice;
\textit{(v)} Feedback; and \textit{(vi)} Questioning
\cite{allison2015making}. These principles are
interrelated elements of teaching rather than mutually exclusive task
classes. They provide a functional description of what a tutor response
may need to accomplish: \textit{(i)} sustaining appropriate cognitive
effort; \textit{(ii)} clarifying knowledge;
\textit{(iii)} demonstrating application; \textit{(iv)} creating
opportunities to practise; \textit{(v)} using learner performance to
guide improvement; and \textit{(vi)} eliciting evidence of
understanding.

Our capability model also draws on research on adaptive scaffolding.
Van de Pol \textit{et al.} distinguish the means of support---including
questioning; explaining; modelling; and feedback---from four properties
that make support contingent on the learner:
\textit{(i)} diagnosis of current competence;
\textit{(ii)} calibrated assistance; \textit{(iii)} fading; and
\textit{(iv)} transfer of responsibility
\cite{vandepol2010scaffolding}. This distinction motivates separating
\textit{(i)} pedagogical-strategy selection from scaffolding calibration;
and \textit{(ii)} observation of a learner's current state from diagnosis
of the cause of an error or impasse.

Evidence-centered design further requires assessment developers to link
claims about latent capabilities to tasks and observable evidence
\cite{mislevy2003ecd}. Contemporary validity theory similarly treats
content review as one component of a broader argument about the intended
interpretation and use of assessment results \cite{reeves2016validity}.
Following these foundations, we organize tutor quality into six
capabilities: \textit{(i)} subject-matter accuracy and grounding;
\textit{(ii)} learner-state modelling;
\textit{(iii)} pedagogical-strategy selection;
\textit{(iv)} scaffolding calibration; \textit{(v)} diagnosis; and
\textit{(vi)} clear and supportive communication appropriate to the
learner's age. The
principles specify response-level pedagogical requirements, whereas the
capabilities define the qualities observed by the rubric.

\subsection{Benchmarks for LLM Tutoring}

Existing tutoring benchmarks operationalize tutoring quality in
substantially different ways. MathTutorBench organizes seven tasks under
three groups: \textit{(i)} subject expertise;
\textit{(ii)} student understanding; and
\textit{(iii)} pedagogical response generation
\cite{macina2025mathtutorbench}. Structured outputs use
task-specific metrics, whereas open-ended tutor responses are evaluated
with a learned preference model. This makes the solver--tutor distinction
explicit and avoids reducing all tutoring ability to answer accuracy.

KMP-Bench provides the closest structural precedent for our turn-level
candidates \cite{shi2026kmpbench}. Its KMP-Dialogue module truncates a
dialogue before a tutor turn and associates each planned tutor action
with one or two of the six pedagogical principles. A candidate and a
reference response are compared using four general criteria plus three
criteria for every assigned principle, with one of three criterion-level
judgments: \textit{(i)} Win; \textit{(ii)} Tie; or
\textit{(iii)} Lose. Its separate KMP-Skills module uses different,
task-appropriate metrics for \textit{(i)} problem solving;
\textit{(ii)} error handling; and \textit{(iii)} problem generation.
The principles therefore select relevant aspects of
dialogue quality rather than act as mutually exclusive benchmark tasks.

TutorBench instead contains 1,490 expert-curated high-school and Advanced
Placement STEM samples covering \textit{(i)} adaptive explanation;
\textit{(ii)} assessment and feedback; and
\textit{(iii)} active-learning support \cite{srinivasa2025tutorbench}.
Each sample has its own atomic criteria, weighted by the importance and
direction of the requirement, and an LLM judge evaluates each criterion
independently. This provides diagnostic granularity but requires a large
sample-specific rubric-authoring effort.

These designs establish three lessons for our benchmark:
\textit{(i)} correctness and tutoring quality must be measured
separately; \textit{(ii)} a reference response is a comparator rather
than the only valid wording; and \textit{(iii)} four constructs play
distinct roles: tasks; pedagogical principles; tutor capabilities; and
rubrics. We
therefore \textit{(i)} use one next-response generation task;
\textit{(ii)} represent pedagogical demand through per-candidate
principle requirements; and \textit{(iii)} use capabilities to organize
observable scoring criteria.

\subsection{Vietnamese and Domain-Specific Educational Evaluation}

Vietnamese LLM evaluation has expanded beyond translated general-purpose
tests. VNHSGE collects more than 19,000 multiple-choice questions and 300
literary essays from Vietnamese high-school examinations across nine
subjects \cite{dao2023vnhsge}. ViMath-Bench focuses on Vietnamese
mathematical reasoning \cite{nguyen2024vimath}, while VMLU evaluates
four areas: \textit{(i)} general knowledge;
\textit{(ii)} reading comprehension; \textit{(iii)} reasoning; and
\textit{(iv)} general multi-turn conversation. Its subject inventory
includes applied
Informatics and several university-level computing areas
\cite{bui2025vmlu}. These resources establish language- and
curriculum-specific evaluation, but their primary units are examination
answers, reasoning outputs, or general conversations rather than
context-conditioned tutor responses.

Vietnamese pedagogical evaluation has also begun to emerge. Outside the
peer-reviewed literature, the publicly announced DeepEduBench initiative
reports 13,700 Vietnamese items used to assess 52 models on
\textit{(i)} stepwise explanation; \textit{(ii)} learner adaptation;
\textit{(iii)} example and exercise selection; and
\textit{(iv)} focused feedback \cite{most2026deepedubench}. We use this
source only to document the initiative's existence and stated scope, not
to infer its methodology. A separate workshop paper evaluates LLMs as
tutors for Vietnamese K--12 learners using 540 yes/no questions in
\textit{(i)} mathematics; \textit{(ii)} physics; and
\textit{(iii)} chemistry \cite{nguyen2025vietnamtutor}. The available
descriptions do not report a benchmark slice for the national
lower-secondary Informatics curriculum, candidates reconstructed from
ordinary teacher-authored dialogues, or a next-response protocol
combining pedagogical principles with tutor capabilities.

The domain-specific literature closes a different part of the gap.
CSTutorBench evaluates 11 models as tutors in a middle-school
block-programming and robotics environment through 17 scenario-based
questions and an eight-criterion pedagogical rubric
\cite{lane2026cstutorbench}. Shin \textit{et al.} instead analyze 249
authentic CodeHelp exchanges from an undergraduate C++ course using
criteria for \textit{(i)} mistake identification;
\textit{(ii)} mistake location; \textit{(iii)} guidance;
\textit{(iv)} accuracy; and \textit{(v)} next steps
\cite{shin2026psychometric}. Multilingual
programming work has also generated exercises in Vietnamese, but does not
evaluate dialogue-level tutoring quality \cite{jordan2024programming}.
Thus, prior work covers Vietnamese pedagogical evaluation and
computing-specific tutor evaluation separately. Our literature search
found no publicly documented benchmark that combines
\textit{(i)} Vietnamese response generation;
\textit{(ii)} the Grades 6--9 Informatics curriculum;
\textit{(iii)} conversation-conditioned next-turn tutoring; and
\textit{(iv)} candidates derived from traceable teacher-authored
pedagogical dialogues. We therefore position this work at that specific
intersection.
