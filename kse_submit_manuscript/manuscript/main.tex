\begin{abstract}
Evaluating a large language model as a tutor requires more than checking
answer correctness: the model must interpret the learner's state, select an
appropriate pedagogical move, and provide calibrated support. We present
\benchmarkname, a Vietnamese Grades~6--9 Informatics AI-tutor benchmark
constructed through a traceable pipeline from 1,050 teacher-authored
dialogues. The pipeline audits
the raw dialogues, establishes a measurement foundation of six pedagogical
principles and six tutor capabilities, and converts every eligible tutor turn
into a context-conditioned next-response candidate. It yields 2,028 candidates
from 665 retained dialogue families. A grounded LLM scores the necessity of all six principles for each
candidate; deterministic checks then retain 1,400 candidates across 655
families. We further define an evaluation framework that provides the required
principles to the tutor model, preserves native multi-turn roles, and compares
the generated response with the teacher-authored response using four common
criteria, three criteria per required principle, and criterion-level
Win/Tie/Lose judgments. Across 4,200 generated responses and 8,400 blind
comparisons, two LLM judges consistently rank Llama~4~Maverick below both
Gemini configurations, while their ordering of the two Gemini configurations
differs. Together, the construction pipeline and evaluation framework provide
a curriculum-grounded protocol for Vietnamese Informatics tutoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly capable of producing
correct answers and fluent explanations, but these abilities do not by
themselves establish that a model can tutor effectively. Tutoring also
requires \textit{(i)} interpreting what a learner currently understands;
\textit{(ii)} diagnosing errors when evidence permits;
\textit{(iii)} selecting an appropriate pedagogical move;
\textit{(iv)} calibrating the amount of support; and
\textit{(v)} communicating in a way that preserves the learner's
opportunity to think. Recent tutoring benchmarks
support this distinction. MathTutorBench reports that mathematical
problem-solving ability does not directly translate into pedagogical
quality \cite{macina2025mathtutorbench}; KMP-Bench separates verifiable
mathematical skills from principle-guided dialogue quality
\cite{shi2026kmpbench}; and TutorBench evaluates responses with
fine-grained, sample-specific criteria \cite{srinivasa2025tutorbench}.
Evaluating a tutor is therefore an assessment-design problem rather than
only a question-answering problem.

Prior AI-tutoring benchmarks leave three gaps for our target setting.
First, their domain coverage is concentrated in mathematics or broad
STEM curricula, while specialized school subjects remain less explored.
Second, they operate predominantly in English, leaving language- and
curriculum-specific tutoring behavior underrepresented. Third, data
construction is not unified: \textit{(i)} KMP-Bench builds pedagogically
planned dialogue flows; \textit{(ii)} TutorBench uses expert-authored
situations and rubrics; and \textit{(iii)} MathTutorBench assembles
existing tutoring datasets under predefined tasks and metrics. In each
case, prior assumptions about tasks or
pedagogical situations structure the data before evaluation. Less is
known about starting from ordinary, teacher-authored pedagogical
dialogues collected independently of a benchmark taxonomy and converting
them into traceable evaluation units.

We address these gaps by constructing \benchmarkname, a Vietnamese
secondary-school Informatics AI-tutor benchmark for Grades~6--9. Rather than generating
conversations from predefined benchmark situations, our construction and
evaluation process:
\begin{itemize}
    \item begins with raw teacher-authored dialogues and audits their
    structural, curricular, and pedagogical quality;
    \item converts eligible tutor turns into traceable,
    context-conditioned next-response candidate samples;
    \item scores the requirement of all six pedagogical principles for
    every candidate, then applies deterministic filtering and locking
    rules to construct the completed benchmark set; and
    \item combines Allison and Tharby's six pedagogical principles
    \cite{allison2015making} with six tutor capabilities to construct
    context-specific rubrics and an AI-tutor evaluation protocol.
\end{itemize}

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item an automated and traceable construction pipeline that transforms
    raw teacher-authored dialogues into benchmark candidates and then
    completed benchmark samples through auditing, pedagogical-principle
    assignment, deterministic filtering, and locking;
    \item a context-specific rubric library and evaluation method that
    combines common criteria with criteria associated with each required
    pedagogical principle to evaluate AI tutors; and
    \item \benchmarkname, a Vietnamese Grades~6--9 Informatics tutoring
    benchmark comprising 1,400 completed samples across 655 raw dialogues.
\end{enumerate}
Together, these contributions establish a curriculum-grounded and
traceable basis for evaluating AI tutoring responses with
\benchmarkname{} in Vietnamese secondary-school Informatics.

\section{Related Work and Background}

\subsection{Pedagogical and Measurement Foundations}

Allison and Tharby's six principles describe complementary teaching
functions rather than mutually exclusive task classes \cite{allison2015making}.
Adaptive-scaffolding research further distinguishes pedagogical moves from
contingent support for a learner's current competence \cite{vandepol2010scaffolding}.
Evidence-centered design and contemporary validity theory motivate linking
intended tutor capabilities to observable evidence and expert review
\cite{mislevy2003ecd,reeves2016validity}. Accordingly, we use pedagogical
principles to specify response-level requirements and six tutor capabilities
to organize observable rubric criteria.

\subsection{General AI-Tutoring Benchmarks}

Existing benchmarks show that answer correctness alone does not capture
tutoring quality. MathTutorBench distinguishes subject expertise, learner
understanding, and pedagogical response generation \cite{macina2025mathtutorbench};
KMP-Bench evaluates a tutor turn in dialogue context with
principle-guided criteria \cite{shi2026kmpbench}; and TutorBench uses
fine-grained, sample-specific criteria \cite{srinivasa2025tutorbench}. These
benchmarks motivate context-conditioned evaluation and explicit pedagogical
requirements, but do not address our curriculum and data-construction setting.

\subsection{Vietnamese and Computing-Specific Educational Evaluation}

Vietnamese evaluation resources establish language- and curriculum-aware
assessment, but primarily evaluate examination answers, reasoning, or general
conversation rather than context-conditioned tutor responses
\cite{dao2023vnhsge,nguyen2024vimath,bui2025vmlu}. Vietnamese tutoring work
has examined K--12 science questions, while computing-focused studies assess
tutoring in block programming, robotics, or undergraduate programming
contexts \cite{nguyen2025vietnamtutor,lane2026cstutorbench,shin2026psychometric,jordan2024programming}.
Thus, Vietnamese educational evaluation and computing-specific tutor evaluation
have been addressed separately. Our literature search found no publicly
documented benchmark combining Vietnamese Grades~6--9 Informatics,
conversation-conditioned next-response evaluation, and traceable candidates
derived from teacher-authored pedagogical dialogues.

\section{Benchmark Construction Pipeline}

label, filter, and collect candidate benchmark samples to build a comprehensive benchmark.

\subsection{Phase 1: Teacher-Authored Dialogues Audit}

Phase~1 audited 1,050 Vietnamese Informatics dialogues for Grades~6--9
authored by the teaching team at Hanoi Metropolitan University (HNMU). To
ground this audit in the national curriculum, we normalized 154 OCR-derived
SGK/SGV Markdown units into 2,750 traceable fragments and
built a rebuildable SQLite full-text search index. Metadata and keyword
retrieval linked SGK evidence to lesson scope and questions, and SGV evidence
to expected answers and explanations.

The hybrid audit combined code-based checks of coverage, required fields,
speaker labels, lesson mapping, and duplicate risk with a semantic and
pedagogical review led by the dedicated AI agent named \texttt{hnmu-dialogue-auditor}.
This specialist AI agent used \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} at medium reasoning effort to
assess an 18-criterion checklist covering structure (3 criteria), consistency (7 criteria),
pedagogy (6 criteria), and duplicate risk (2 criteria). For each dialogue, it used the raw
fields, retrieved SGK/SGV fragments, and HNMU's cognitive-level and
scaffolding guidance to record criterion-level decisions, confidence,
evidence, and reviewer actions. Code
validated and merged the lesson-based shards, then applied a strict rule:
any \textit{failed} criterion yielded \textit{failed}; otherwise any \textit{uncertain}
yielded \textit{need human review}; all remaining dialogues \textit{passed}.
This retained 665 dialogues, while routing 382 to review and rejecting 3.

\subsection{Phase 2: Pedagogical Measurement Foundation}
\label{sec:phase2-pedagogical-measurement}

We distinguish response-level pedagogical \emph{functions} from tutor
\emph{capabilities}. The functional layer adopts Allison and Tharby's six
interrelated principles: Challenge; Explanation; Modelling; Practice;
Feedback; and Questioning \cite{allison2015making}. KMP-Bench operationalizes
these principles for tutor-response evaluation \cite{shi2026kmpbench}. They
respectively capture productive cognitive demand; conceptual clarification;
demonstration of application; opportunities for independent practice;
response grounded in learner work; and questions whose answers advance the
interaction.

The six-capability model synthesizes complementary evidence. MathTutorBench
distinguishes subject expertise, learner understanding, and pedagogical
response generation \cite{macina2025mathtutorbench}. KMP-Bench evaluates both
skill correctness and principle-guided dialogue behavior
\cite{shi2026kmpbench}. TutorBench contributes adaptive explanation,
assessment and feedback, and active-learning requirements expressed through
atomic criteria \cite{srinivasa2025tutorbench}. Adaptive-scaffolding research
motivates separating strategy selection from the calibration of support and
treating learner diagnosis as an input to contingent assistance
\cite{vandepol2010scaffolding}. HNMU's document about scaffolding method supplies local
expectations for diagnosing the learner's current state and gradually
scaffolding support. Evidence-centered design motivates linking every
capability to observable response evidence \cite{mislevy2003ecd}.

Accordingly, we define six tutor capabilities: \textit{(i)} subject-matter
accuracy and grounding; \textit{(ii)} learner-state modelling;
\textit{(iii)} pedagogical-strategy selection; \textit{(iv)} scaffolding
calibration; \textit{(v)} diagnosis of errors, misconceptions, and missing
prerequisites; and \textit{(vi)} clear, supportive, age-appropriate
communication. Boundary analysis separates learner-state description from
causal diagnosis, and strategy selection from the amount, timing, and transfer
of support. The principles activate response-specific instructions and
criteria; the tutor capabilities organize the observable rubric criteria.

\subsection{Phase 3: From Raw Dialogues to Benchmark Data}

Phase~3 combines the audited dialogues with the pedagogical foundation from
Phase~2 to construct \benchmarkname{} samples. It comprises three operations:
deterministic turn-level conversion, grounded principle-requirement scoring
with code-based quality gates, and collection of the eligible benchmark pool.

\subsubsection{Turn-level candidate construction}
The 665 retained dialogues were converted deterministically by treating every
tutor turn as a possible reference response. The initial student turn becomes
the prompt; intervening utterances retain their student or tutor roles in the
conversation history; and the selected tutor turn becomes the reference
response. Later turns are excluded, while the source question and teacher-provided answer are kept
separately as subject-matter grounding. This policy produces 2,028 candidates
across 665 dialogue families, including multiple history depths from the same
source. For example, a six-turn raw dialogue
\(D_1 = [S_1,T_1,S_2,T_2,S_3,T_3]\) yields 3 candidates:
\(C_1=[S_1;\varnothing;T_1]\),
\(C_2=[S_1;(T_1,S_2);T_2]\), and
\(C_3=[S_1;(T_1,S_2,T_2,S_3);T_3]\), where each bracket records the prompt,
history, and reference response, respectively. Here, \(S_i\) and \(T_i\)
denote student and tutor turns. All three candidates retain the same family ID
for tracing and data splitting.

\subsubsection{LLM-assisted principle scoring and filtering}
For each candidate, we use Gemini~3.5~Flash to evaluate the necessity of all six
principles from the information available before the target tutor response:
grade, lesson, target position, Bloom level, student prompt, role-preserving
history, source question, and teacher-provided answer. The reference response
is deliberately excluded, preventing the selection of evaluation criteria
from being conditioned on the response against which the evaluated AI tutor will
later be compared.

The model assigns a five-point necessity score to each principle. A score of 1
means that the principle is irrelevant or counterproductive in the given
context. A score of 2 indicates only a weak relationship to the learner's
need. A score of 3 represents a pedagogically valid but optional strategy. A
score of 4 means that the principle is required for a good next response. A
score of 5 is reserved for a principle that is essential to the central
pedagogical need. Code, rather than the LLM, selects principles scoring at
least 4 and applies schema validation, thresholding, semantic linting, and
eligibility gates.

\subsubsection{Eligible benchmark pool and descriptive statistics}
The full scoring run contains 12,168 principle scores for all 2,028
candidates. We define a candidate as \emph{eligible} only when it has one to
three required principles, all cited evidence is traceable to the supplied
context, and every score of 4 or 5 is supported by both an independent
pedagogical need and a counterfactual showing that this need would remain
unmet without the principle. Eligible candidates must also contain no
optionality conflict among required principles: in particular, Feedback must guide improvement beyond
mere confirmation or praise, while Questioning must make a learner answer
necessary rather than merely useful. Finally, the required-principle set must
occur in at least five candidates across at least three dialogue families.

These gates retain 1,400 eligible candidates from 655 raw dialogues passed in
Phase~1 as the current \benchmarkname{} benchmark set and exclude the remaining
628. Exclusion flags may overlap: 592 candidates reduce
Feedback to confirmation or praise; 20 contain an inconsistent score-4/5
rationale; 10 assign Questioning without establishing answer dependency; 8
have no principle scoring at least 4; 7 have an insufficiently supported
principle combination; and one requires more than three principles.
Figure~\ref{fig:phase3-statistics} summarizes principle incidence, grade,
turns per sample, and characters per turn.

\section{Evaluation Framework}

Following the KMP-Dialogue structure \cite{shi2026kmpbench}, our evaluation
framework for \benchmarkname{} separates tutor-input preparation, criterion
construction, and blind pairwise assessment.

\subsection{Context Loading and Tutor-Response Generation}

The tutor model receives a system instruction containing its Vietnamese
secondary-school Informatics tutoring role, grade, lesson, source task, a
common tutoring instruction, and the exact set of required principles.
The dialogue itself is sent through the provider's native multi-turn
interface: the initial student prompt and subsequent student turns use the
\texttt{user} role, while prior tutor turns use the
\texttt{assistant}/\texttt{model} role. The final message is always the
student turn to be answered. Benchmark sample IDs, principle scores, criteria,
learning-resource fragments, the teacher-provided answer, and the reference
response are not shown to the tutor model.

\subsection{Two-Tier Rubric Construction}

Six tutor capabilities, as defined in Section~\ref{sec:phase2-pedagogical-measurement},
provide a coverage map for four common criteria: \textit{(i)} subject-matter
accuracy and verifiability; \textit{(ii)} alignment with the learner state and
immediate goal; \textit{(iii)} calibrated support that preserves meaningful
learner work; and \textit{(iv)} clear, respectful, age-appropriate
communication. Each of the six principles contributes three criteria that
measure its incremental pedagogical function. The resulting catalog contains
4 common and 18 principle-specific criteria. A benchmark sample requiring \(n\)
principles is evaluated with \(4+3n\) criteria, providing a stable common core
while adapting the remaining criteria to its pedagogical demands.

\subsection{Blind Pairwise Judging and Score Aggregation}

In this procedure, a judge receives the dialogue context, source question,
teacher-provided answer, all applicable criteria, and two anonymous responses:
the generated tutor response and the
teacher-authored reference. Response order is deterministically randomized and
restored only after judging. For every criterion, the judge selects
\textit{response 1}, \textit{response 2}, or \textit{Tie}; code then maps the
decision back to the target model as Win, Lose, or Tie.
Let \(W_r,T_r,L_r\) be the numbers of Win, Tie, and Lose judgments for
criterion \(r\). Its win rate is
\[
 A_r = \frac{W_r}{W_r+T_r+L_r}.
\]

\section{Experiments and Analysis}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Metrics}: Each generated response is compared with the teacher-authored
reference by two separate LLM judges: Gemini~3.5~Flash with \texttt{thinking\_level=MEDIUM}, and the
\texttt{gpt-5.4-mini-2026-03-17} snapshot with medium reasoning effort. Both
use the same prompt, an 8,192-token output cap, the applicable rubric set,
anonymous response order, and identical deterministic post-processing. The key metrics include:
\textit{(i)} Holistic Win rate (from the Win/Tie/Lose holistic judgment);
\textit{(ii)} General-Level Accuracy (average of the four common criteria);
\textit{(iii)} Principle-Level Accuracy (average of the three criteria for each required principle);
and \textit{(iv)} Overall Accuracy (average of General-Level Accuracy and the mean of Principle-Level scores).
Furthermore, because multiple benchmark samples can originate from the same raw dialogue, we additionally report a
family-macro Overall score that first gives each of the 655 dialogue families
equal weight. Confidence intervals (CIs) and pairwise differences use 5,000
cluster-bootstrap resamples over \texttt{sample\_id}; 4,995 resamples contain
observations for all six principles.

\textbf{Models}: The tutor panel contains \textit{(i)} Gemini~3.5~Flash with the baseline
instruction; \textit{(ii)} the same base model with a LearnLM-oriented
educational instruction; and \textit{(iii)} Llama~4~Maverick. The first two are
therefore instruction configurations rather than distinct base models. Both
Gemini configurations use \texttt{thinking\_level=MEDIUM} without returned
thoughts. All three configurations omit sampling overrides and use a
2,048-token output cap. Each configuration generates one response for every
sample, yielding 4,200 tutor responses on 1,400 benchmark samples.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\subsection{What the Evaluation Distinguishes}

The intersection of the two judge panels provides the most defensible result:
both judges, candidate-level aggregation, family-macro aggregation, and all
four clustered Gemini--Llama comparisons support the same coarse ordering.
This is evidence that the context-specific rubric can detect a comparatively
large performance difference in the evaluated panel. The conclusion is not
dependent on one judge or on over-represented dialogue families.

The experiment does not establish three absolute levels of tutor quality.
Model names are not controlled quality labels, and no human-locked set of
good, medium, and poor responses was used. The reversed baseline--LearnLM
ordering further shows that the framework does not yet resolve small
instruction-level differences consistently across judges. Accordingly,
Overall is a relative pairwise score under this protocol, not an absolute
certificate of tutoring quality.

\subsection{Measurement Implications}

The detailed results expose where the evaluation framework is more and less
stable. Explanation and Modelling show comparatively high cross-judge
agreement, while Questioning and the common criteria require further
calibration. The extreme scarcity of Challenge and Practice also means that
their principle-level scores have high conceptual importance but weak
empirical support. Since the KMP-compatible Overall score macro-weights all
six principles equally, Challenge with eight candidates receives the same
principle-level weight as Explanation with 863 candidates. The
KMP-compatible score is useful for methodological comparability
\cite{shi2026kmpbench}, but should be accompanied by principle support and
family-sensitive results.

\subsection{Threats to Validity and Next Validation}

Several limitations constrain interpretation. First, both judges are LLMs;
their agreement measures cross-judge stability rather than correctness against
independent human ground truth. A stratified human evaluation should focus on
the common accuracy, communication, and scaffolding criteria, Questioning, and
cases with directional judge disagreement.

Second, Gemini judges two responses produced by the same model family and is
substantially harsher than GPT toward Llama. This same-family relationship is a
potential confound, particularly because the Gemini judge observes a
35.84--38.36 point Gemini--Llama gap while GPT observes a 9.45--10.22 point
gap. Third, Gemini exhibits a descriptive preference for response 1. A
controlled swap-order evaluation on the same stratified response pairs is
needed to separate position effects from response quality.

Fourth, the \textit{gold-answer-only v4} contract deliberately excludes
raw-audit learning fragments because they were not reliable at candidate
level. Although \texttt{gold\_answer} provides a cleaner subject-matter
anchor, it may not cover multiple correct procedures, interface or code
variants, or pedagogically valuable strategies that differ from the
teacher-authored reference. This limitation is especially relevant to the
65.55\% agreement on subject-matter accuracy.

Finally, the rubric catalog and model-assigned required-principle sets require
further expert calibration. The principle set is activated by a single
Gemini requirement-scoring run at \texttt{requirement\_score >= 4}; an
activation error changes which principle-specific criteria are applied.
Further validation should therefore calibrate the rubric boundaries without
editing them merely to maximize judge agreement, review the activated
principles, and add Challenge, Practice, and Modelling cases before making
fine-grained principle-level claims.
\section{Conclusion}

We introduced \benchmarkname, a Vietnamese Grades~6--9 Informatics tutoring
benchmark constructed from teacher-authored dialogues through auditable
screening, turn-level conversion, grounded pedagogical-requirement scoring,
and deterministic eligibility gates. Its 1,400 samples support native
multi-turn tutor generation and context-specific evaluation with four common
criteria plus three criteria per required principle. Across three tutor
configurations and two separately reported LLM judges, the framework
consistently distinguishes the larger Gemini--Llama performance gap, while the
Gemini baseline--LearnLM ordering remains judge-dependent. These results
support relative discrimination of large differences, not absolute quality
certification; expert calibration and controlled position-bias testing remain
necessary next steps.
